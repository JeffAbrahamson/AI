# Evidence behind the style code

Read this when deciding how much weight a rule deserves, or when the
author asks why the skill recommends something. It records what the
research supports, what it does not, and which rules are editorial
judgements rather than findings.

## Goal

The code covers purpose, evidence, argument, voice and visuals. For
the author's subjects (mobility, nonprofit governance, and the politics
they sometimes touch), effectiveness means reaching relevant people and
helping them understand or reconsider an issue, while building the
author's credibility over time. Raw reach is not the goal.

## What the evidence supports

| Finding | Implication for the code | Limit of the evidence |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn says it is reducing generic content and engagement bait, and accepts AI assistance when the result retains the author's perspective. | Preserve the author's analysis, experience and voice. Avoid interchangeable "thought leadership" and artificial invitations to comment. | These are platform statements, not independent measurements of ranking effects. |
| Web usability experiments found benefits from concise, scannable, nonpromotional writing. | Put substance early, use coherent paragraphs and cut promotional language. | This research concerns reading and usability, not LinkedIn distribution. |
| Experiments on communicating uncertainty found relatively small credibility costs, particularly when uncertainty was quantified. | Preserve material qualifications; make them informative rather than vaguely hesitant. | This does not establish that every caveat improves trust. |
| Political out-group language was associated with substantially more sharing on Facebook and Twitter. | Treat engagement cautiously: a polarising post can spread without demonstrating persuasion. | The study was observational, concerned US politics, and did not examine LinkedIn. |

There is also evidence on **conversational receptiveness**: language
showing thoughtful engagement with an opposing view can improve
perceived persuasiveness and willingness to collaborate. Applying that
finding to a LinkedIn post (including a French one) is a reasonable
editorial inference, not a demonstrated platform effect.

## Durable rules versus distribution hypotheses

Distinguish **durable editorial rules** (clarity, evidence, fairness,
voice) from **distribution hypotheses** (length, format, posting time,
hashtags). Even large benchmark reports acknowledge observational data,
selected samples and substantial differences between accounts. They
provide things to test, not universal prescriptions.

No study establishes that the skill's exact combination of rules
maximises reach. The rules are editorial choices informed by the
research and the author's objectives.

## Visuals

A photograph can document one situation; it cannot establish how common
that situation is. LinkedIn supports alt text on images for
accessibility.
